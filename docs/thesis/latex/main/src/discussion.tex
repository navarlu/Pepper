\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The study has several limitations that constrain how strongly the results in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} can be read.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The comparison is not an isolated LLM test.}
    Condition~A and Condition~B differ in three components at once --- speech-to-text, language model, and text-to-speech --- so any subjective difference between the two conditions cannot be attributed to the language model alone.
    The cloud cascade pairs \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} with the \texttt{nova} voice and \texttt{gpt-4o-mini-transcribe}, both of which are plausibly better than the local Piper voice and \texttt{faster-whisper} on this audience.
    The results should therefore be read as a comparison of two complete local and cloud voice cascades on the same task, rather than as an isolated comparison of the language models.

    \item \textbf{Sample size and condition assignment.}
    Of the 26 questionnaire responses, 20 could be matched back to a specific session, 10 per condition (the remaining 6 carried no participant identifier). Ten respondents per condition is too small to generalize beyond this study, so the per-condition tables in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} are reported as observations from this deployment rather than broader claims.
    The condition was alternated rather than fully randomised across visitors (see \secref{sec:experiment_loop}), so a residual confound with time-of-day or visitor population on the deployment day cannot be ruled out.

    \item \textbf{Self-selection.}
    Visitors who chose to engage with the robot are likely more positively disposed toward robots than the average passer-by.
    This is expected to inflate subjective ratings across both conditions and limits how far the absolute scores can be generalized.

    \item \textbf{Recruitment switch.}
    Passive recruitment did not produce questionnaire responses, so the experimenter actively approached visitors and briefly explained the study (see \secref{sec:exp_recruitment}).
    This likely improved task discoverability --- the guided-versus-self-directed observation in \secref{sec:exp_field} shows that briefed visitors had smoother conversations --- but it also primed the visitors' expectations of what Pepper can do and shifted the kinds of questions they asked.

    \item \textbf{Retrieval quality was not separately evaluated.}
    The \texttt{query\_search} tool was rarely invoked in the field deployment (\secref{sec:tool_surface}) because visitor questions almost all landed on the four live-endpoint tools.
    The retrieval-augmented question answering called for in the thesis assignment is therefore present as a working component but not benchmarked in isolation; a small offline evaluation over the seeded document corpus --- example questions, the retrieved chunks, and whether the answer was correct --- would close this gap and remains a natural follow-up item.

    \item \textbf{Gesture evaluation is rough.}
    The custom Likert item asks whether Pepper's gestures \emph{felt natural and fit what she was saying}, which is a perceived-appropriateness rating, not a detailed measure of how well each gesture's meaning matched the spoken text.
    Because gestures fire on tool calls rather than on the streamed reply (\secref{sec:dec_streaming}), the visible vocabulary is also dominated by ``thinking'' and ``looking up'' gestures, so the item should be read as evidence about that subset of the design rather than about the full gesture surface.
\end{itemize}


\section{Implications}
\label{sec:implications}

The two backends pull in opposite directions on every axis that matters for a real deployment.
Condition~B was perceived as more pleasant to interact with; Condition~A was the quicker one to respond, and the only one that keeps the visitor's audio on local hardware.
The rest of this section discusses each of these in detail.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Why B was preferred} --- On general-knowledge and reasoning benchmarks, \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} sits noticeably above Llama~3.1~8B Instruct (about 13 points on MMLU and 15 points on HumanEval~\cite{openai2024gpt4omini,grattafiori2024llama3}, around 100 Elo points in the Chatbot Arena), and the cloud cascade also pairs it with a smoother synthesized voice (\texttt{nova}) and a more accurate speech-to-text model. A more capable language model with a warmer voice and cleaner transcripts is expected to be rated higher, and on this small sample that is what happens: the gap is consistent in direction (0.1--0.3 points on the Godspeed subscales, up to 0.8 on the gesture item) but small in size. It is best read as a tendency rather than a proven difference.

    \item \textbf{The latency paradox} --- Condition~A was descriptively about twice as fast as Condition~B on the median session, yet Condition~B was rated slightly higher on the speed item (4.30 vs 3.80). There are three plausible reasons, not mutually exclusive. First, both medians (1.56~s and 3.53~s) fall inside a range that feels conversational, so neither is heard as a delay. Second, the two cascades differ in speech-to-text and text-to-speech components as well as in the language model (see the LLM-isolation limitation in \secref{sec:limitations}); the cloud cascade's smoother voice and more accurate transcripts plausibly shape \emph{perceived} speed independently of the measured first-audio time. Third, plain sampling noise: with ten respondents per condition, a 0.5-point gap could easily come from random variation alone. Either way the practical message is the same --- at this scale, raw measured latency was not what made Pepper feel fast or slow.

    \item \textbf{Cost} --- The two backends have opposite cost shapes.

    The cloud cascade is pay-as-you-go: every minute of conversation is billed against the OpenAI API, and an hour with no visitors costs nothing. At prices accessed on 18~May~2026~\cite{openai_pricing}, the ten Condition~B sessions in this study corresponded to approximately USD~0.29 per active conversation hour. The bill was computed by \texttt{docs/thesis/experiment/\allowbreak evaluation/cost/\allowbreak compute\_b\_costs.py} against the official OpenAI usage export. OpenAI pricing is model-dependent and changes over time, so this estimate should be interpreted as a snapshot rather than a stable long-term price.

    The local cascade has no per-token API fee, but it is not free: it requires up-front hardware, plus ongoing electricity, maintenance, administrator time, GPU opportunity cost, and the effort of pulling in model updates. The remainder of this paragraph focuses on the hardware up-front cost, which dominates at the usage scale of a faculty reception; the other items are smaller and harder to attribute and are left out of the comparison. The GPU used here, an NVIDIA RTX~4000~SFF Ada Generation, lists for about USD~1{,}250~\cite{nvidia_rtx4000_sff}. Dividing the GPU price by the cloud per-hour rate gives a break-even of roughly 4{,}170 hours of active conversation. Depending on how much the robot is actively used --- between two and six hours of conversation per day is a reasonable range for a faculty reception --- the GPU pays for itself in roughly two to six years.

    \item \textbf{Upgradability} --- Switching the cloud cascade to a different language model is a one-line configuration change: the \texttt{model} string can be swapped for any other OpenAI-hosted model, or the LLM component can be replaced with a plugin for a different provider for example Anthropic. This makes it cheap to follow frontier improvements.

    Upgrading the local cascade is more involved. Moving to a 32B or 70B language model means either accepting that it does not fit on the existing GPU alongside the speech components or buying a larger GPU. The local backend is therefore conservative by construction: it follows the slow tail of open-weight improvements at the size class it can fit.

    \item \textbf{Data sovereignty} --- The cost and upgradability advantages of the cloud cascade come with one non-negotiable downside: every visitor utterance, transcript, and reply leaves the deployment site and is processed on the provider's infrastructure. Whether that is acceptable is a deployment decision and depends on the institution. The local cascade keeps speech recognition, dialogue generation, and speech synthesis on hardware controlled by the operator; the one current exception is that \texttt{query\_search} computes embeddings through the OpenAI API at ingestion and query time as a convenience choice, so RAG use is not strictly local under Condition~A. The embedder is interchangeable and swapping it for a local model is a one-component change (see \secref{sec:exp_ethics}).

    \item \textbf{Picking a backend} --- The choice between the two backends depends on the needs of the deployment. The trade-offs above --- subjective quality, latency, cost shape, upgrade path, and where the audio is processed --- pull in different directions, and which of them matters most is specific to the application.
\end{itemize}
