This chapter describes the user study that evaluates the receptionist system built in Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}.
The system was deployed at a real reception desk.
Visitors interacted with it, and their experience was measured with a short questionnaire whose answers were linked to the recorded session log via a unique conversation identifier.
The chapter is organized in five parts.
\secref{sec:exp_design} states the research questions and the experimental design.
\secref{sec:exp_procedure} describes the deployment site, the visitor flow, and how participants were recruited.
\secref{sec:exp_questionnaire} covers the questionnaire.
\secref{sec:exp_results} presents the quantitative results: latency and subjective ratings.
\secref{sec:exp_field} closes the chapter with the qualitative observations made by the experimenter during the deployment.
Interpretation of these results, study limitations, and proposed remedies are taken up in Chapters~\ref{ch:discussion} and~\ref{ch:future_work}.

\section{Research Questions and Design}
\label{sec:exp_design}

The study addresses two research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} How does an LLM-driven receptionist on the Pepper robot perform in a real institutional setting, both in terms of objective interaction quality (latency) and in terms of the subjective experience reported by visitors?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} How do the two voice backends---Condition~A (the locally hosted cascade) and Condition~B (the cloud-hosted cascade)---compare in the same deployment and on the same task?
\end{itemize}

The study uses a \textbf{between-subjects} design, because an interaction with a receptionist is naturally a single visit. And the subjective experience of interacting with Pepper in one condition would likely influence the experience of interacting with her in the other condition.
Each visitor interacts with the system once, in exactly one of the two conditions, and provides a single questionnaire response.
The condition is assigned by the experimental loop wrapper (see \secref{sec:experiment_loop}), which alternates between Condition~A and Condition~B on each new session.

\section{Procedure}
\label{sec:exp_procedure}

\subsection{Setting}
\label{sec:exp_setting}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.48\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{src/figures/setup.jpeg}
\caption{Setting: the reception desk where the experiment was done.}
\label{fig:setup}
\end{wrapfigure}

The deployment site was the reception of Building~E of FEE CTU at Prague.
Pepper was placed behind the standard reception desk, facing visitors (mostly students and staff) who approach from the entrance corridor.
A pane of glass separates the reception staff side, where Pepper stood, from the visitor side of the desk.
With the microphone on Pepper's side, the glass and the distance made speech transcription unreliable.
So external USB microphone described in \secref{sec:pepper_robot} was mounted on the visitor side of the glass so that speech capture stayed close to the visitor.
A short video of an interaction at the desk is available in the \href{https://github.com/navarlu/Pepper}{project repository}~\cite{github-repo-thesis}.



\subsection{Visitor Flow and Recruitment}
\label{sec:exp_visitor_flow}
\label{sec:exp_recruitment}

The original plan was passive recruitment.
A printed instructions board next to Pepper (Appendix~\ref{ch:instructions}) was meant to be the only briefing: it explained what Pepper can help with, how to talk to her, and how to fill in the questionnaire afterwards.
A visitor would read the board, speak to Pepper, and at the end of the session scan a QR code on Pepper's tablet that opened the questionnaire pre-filled with that session's participant identifier (a short tag of the form \texttt{T07}, which serves as the join key between the questionnaire row and the system log).

However, this did not work.
Several visitors engaged with Pepper during the first hours of the deployment day, but none filled in the questionnaire.
From that point onward the experimenter actively approached visitors near the reception, briefly explained what Pepper could help with, invited them to try her, and asked them to fill in the questionnaire afterwards. All 26 responses were collected on Monday 18~May 2026.

The switch turned out to carry an unexpected benefit: the experimenter was present for every interaction, both the ones that went well and the ones that fell apart.
The systematic observations that came out of this presence are reported in \secref{sec:exp_field}.


\subsection{Ethics and Data Handling}
\label{sec:exp_ethics}

This research was approved by the Committee for Research Ethics at the Czech Technical University in Prague (reference number 0000-20/25/51902/EKČVUT).
Visitors were informed about the study before they spoke to Pepper.
The instructions board next to the desk stated that the conversation would be processed by the system and used for the thesis evaluation (see Appendix~\ref{ch:instructions}), and the experimenter additionally briefed each participant verbally during the active-recruitment phase (see \secref{sec:exp_recruitment}).
The questionnaire was anonymous---it asked for age, gender, and a short participant identifier, but no name---and the system logs do not contain visitor names either, so questionnaire rows and session logs are joined only through that short identifier.
The session logs include the visitor's microphone audio and the agent's synthesized reply alongside the event stream; these, together with the questionnaire responses, are stored on the Raspberry~Pi and in the project's experiment-results folder.

The two backends differ in what leaves the institution.
In Condition~A, the visitor's microphone audio, transcripts, and the agent's replies stay on the Raspberry~Pi and the departmental GPU server.
In Condition~B, every visitor utterance is sent to OpenAI for transcription, language-model inference, and speech synthesis, together with the tool-result text appended to the model context---which for the live-endpoint tools (e.g., \texttt{lookup\_person}) includes the public faculty directory data returned by the lookup.

\section{Questionnaire}
\label{sec:exp_questionnaire}

The questionnaire was hosted on Google Forms.
The full questionnaire is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{ch:questionnaire}; this section describes its structure and the rationale for each block.

The questionnaire is organized into five blocks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identification and demographics}---four items: the participant identifier, self-reported gender, age, and familiarity with robots on a 5-point scale.
    \item \textbf{Custom Likert items}---three 5-point Likert items (``strongly disagree'' to ``strongly agree'') targeting whether the visitor got the information they were looking for, whether Pepper responded quickly enough, and whether her hand and arm gestures felt natural and fit what she was saying.
    \item \textbf{Categorical and open-ended items}---four items about the interaction content and the visitor's perspective: what topic the visitor talked about, what problems occurred during the interaction, what the visitor would like Pepper to be able to help with in future, and whom they would prefer at the reception desk in general.
    \item \textbf{Godspeed scales}---twenty-one items from the Godspeed questionnaire~\cite{bartneck2009godspeed}, presented as bipolar adjective pairs on a 5-point scale, covering four of the five Godspeed subscales (Anthropomorphism, Animacy, Likeability, Perceived Intelligence). Godspeed was chosen because it is the most widely reported scale in the receptionist-robot literature surveyed in Chapter~\ref{ch:related_work}, which keeps the per-subscale means directly comparable to prior studies.
    \item \textbf{Open comment}---a final free-text item that invites the visitor to share anything else about their experience that the structured items did not capture.
\end{itemize}

The fifth Godspeed subscale, Perceived Safety, was \emph{not} included.
Its items measure the respondent's self-reported emotional state as an indirect proxy for how safe the robot feels, which is most informative when there is a real possibility of physical contact with the robot.

\section{Quantitative Results}
\label{sec:exp_results}

The questionnaire collected 26 valid responses on a single deployment day.
Twenty of those 26 responses could be matched back to a specific session log via the participant-identifier mechanism: 10 to a Condition~A (local) session and 10 to a Condition~B (cloud) session.
The remaining 6 responses contained a participant identifier that did not uniquely map to a logged session, because the visitor scanned the printed backup QR after walking away and the on-tablet identifier did not get copied into the form correctly.
These 6 responses are kept in the pooled descriptive statistics but are reported separately under ``unknown'' in the per-condition tables; they are excluded from the direct A--B comparison.
The numbers and figures below were produced by the analysis scripts and notebooks in the project repository~\cite{github-repo-thesis}; subsequent references give each script or notebook by its path relative to the repository root.
The cleaned per-respondent responses, with each row matched to the corresponding session log, are kept at \texttt{docs/thesis/experiment/results/results.csv} in the same repository.


\subsection{Sample}
\label{sec:exp_sample}

The 26 respondents were 22 men and 4 women, with ages ranging from 19 to 49 and a median of 25.5~years.
Self-reported familiarity with robots, on a 5-point scale from ``not familiar at all'' (1) to ``very familiar'' (5), had a mean of 3.62 and a median of 4.
This is the kind of sample one would expect at an engineering-school reception during teaching hours: mostly young men with some prior exposure to robots. The ratings reported below should be read with that audience in mind.


\subsection{Objective Latency}
\label{sec:exp_latency}

The headline objective measurement is the time-to-first-audio (TTFA): the time from the end of the visitor's utterance to the first audio frame submitted to Pepper's audio device.

TTFA is computed per turn from each session's event log, and each session is then summarized by the median TTFA across its turns.
The two conditions are compared on these 10 per-session medians, so each visitor contributes one observation; the full computation is in \texttt{docs/thesis/experiment/evaluation/latency/latency\_per\_session.py} and \texttt{docs/thesis/experiment/evaluation/latency/latency\_histogram.py}.

\tabref{tab:ttfa} reports the per-condition statistics.
Condition~A is descriptively faster: the median session is 1.39~s for the local cascade, compared with 3.30~s for the cloud cascade.

\subsection{Visitor Preferences}
\label{sec:exp_preferences}

One item asked who the visitor would prefer at the reception desk of Building~E.
Of the 26 respondents, 19 (73~\%) preferred Pepper, 4 (15~\%) preferred a human, and 3 (12~\%) answered other.
This number should be read with one site-specific context note: at the time of the deployment the human reception staff at Building~E did not regularly speak English.
For visitors who do not speak Czech, Pepper was therefore not a robot-versus-human comparison but the only English-speaking option at the desk, which plausibly inflates the Pepper preference within that subset.

\section{Observations from Experiment}
\label{sec:exp_field}

Because the experimenter was present for every session, the following recurring issues were noted during the day.
They applied to both conditions equally unless noted otherwise.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pepper's voice behind the glass.} The glass partition attenuated Pepper's voice on the visitor side. Turning the volume up to compensate produced audible echo, so several visitors ended up reading Pepper's words on the live tablet transcript rather than relying on the audio alone.
    \item \textbf{Background chatter.} The reception is located in a busy corridor. The microphone occasionally picked up nearby conversation as if it were the visitor speaking, and Pepper would politely reply to it.
    \item \textbf{Repeated filler on tool retries.} A short ``Let me check'' utterance is played in parallel with each tool call to cover the silence before Pepper's reply. When a tool failed and Pepper retried with a slightly different argument, the same filler line was spoken on every retry---in one case three times in a row---which sounded strongly unnatural. Limiting the filler to fire at most once per visitor turn would be enough to prevent this.
    \item \textbf{Guided versus self-directed visitors.} Visitors who were briefed by the experimenter on what Pepper can help with usually had a smooth conversation. Visitors who approached cold tended to ask for things outside Pepper's capabilities, and the conversation degraded into apologies.
\end{itemize}
